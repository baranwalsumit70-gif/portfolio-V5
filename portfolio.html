01 · SaaS · Recruitment · 2025

Smart HR Companion for Indian IT recruitment

An AI-powered workspace that helps recruiters parse resumes, score candidates, match them to jobs and run screenings.

Role
Product design intern · 6 months
Type
SaaS · Recruitment
Year
2025

Interactive case study ↗Working prototype ↗

Overview

One product, eight modules, six months. Tvarah Pulse turns resumes into living candidate profiles scored, verified and matched to a job before the recruiter's first call.

  • My role: Product Design. Research, flows, UI, prototypes and dev handoff.
  • Timeline: 6 months, 16 Jan to 13 Jul 2026
  • Worked with: Founders, PM intern and developers from PRD to handoff.
  • Tools: Figma, FigJam and AI-assisted prototyping. Design System: Shadcn UI

Problem

Recruiters juggle too many tools to make one hire. Resumes, JDs, interview notes and status updates are spread across spreadsheets, job portals, email and chat so nobody has a clear, real-time view of the pipeline.

  • 4–6 hrs: recruiter time lost every day verifying resumes against LinkedIn and Naukri
  • 56%: of Indian hiring managers saw at least one case of resume fraud in 2024 (NASSCOM)
  • 48 hrs: window before a top passive IT candidate is approached by a competitor
How might we help a recruiter go from a pile of resumes to a confident shortlist in minutes, not hours?

Solution

8 modules, 1 connected hiring workspace:

  • Dashboard: hiring KPIs, pipeline funnel and interviews
  • Candidates: upload and parse resumes, every profile scored by Pulse
  • Jobs: bulk-upload JDs, requirements extracted automatically
  • AI insight: an explainable score with strengths, gaps and flags
  • Matching: candidate to JD fit, ranked and explained
  • Screening: guided questionnaire that ends in a panel decision
  • Ask Tvarah: AI assistant scoped to a candidate, job or pipeline
  • Client portal: clients approve or reject shortlists in one click

Process

  1. ResearchUser interviews with recruiters, competitive analysis of 12 products, product teardown and UI scan, user persona.
  2. DefinePRD with the PM intern, database schema study, information architecture, user flow diagrams.
  3. Design & prototypeDesign system and components, hi-fi screens with every state, AI-based quick prototyping, marketing website.

Final design

Impact

8 modulesOne connected hiring workspace
12 productsBenchmarked across 12 UI pattern areas
6 monthsFrom research to dev-ready screens

Due to the NDA, I'm unable to share the iterations and detailed research at this stage. However, I'd be happy to discuss them in more detail during the interview.

Learnings

Working next to a PM and the database schema taught me to design from the data model outward. Iterating the candidate page from version 1 to version 2 showed how much clarity comes from removing, not adding.

02 · E-commerce · Jewellery · 2026

Angara: Product detail page redesign

A redesigned product detail page for Angara that makes customisation clear, builds trust early and adds value through engraving and gifting.

Role
UX design assignment
Type
E-commerce · Jewellery
Year
2026

Interactive case study ↗Live site ↗

Overview

Angara brings a 400-year legacy in gemstones and jewellery-making online, selling handcrafted, made-to-order pieces with high-quality diamonds, coloured gemstones and pearls. The brief was to find the gaps in the current product detail page and redesign it for mobile and desktop, with a seamless customisation experience.

  • US shoppers, age 25 to 65
  • Male and female buyers in a similar ratio
  • Mobile and desktop
  • Goals: more conversions, more engagement, lower bounce rate, higher satisfaction

Problem

The current page asks buyers to make an expensive, emotional decision with confusing choices. Quality is presented two ways, selections rely on colour alone, the price appears twice, and trust signals like reviews sit far below the fold.

  • Gemstone quality mixes origin (lab-grown, natural) and grade (good to heirloom) in one control
  • Carat and metal selection is shown only through a border or colour, which fails low-vision and colour-blind users
  • The price is displayed twice, and a vague "Change" link and a "New" badge add noise
  • Read more and read less take up space inside cards
  • Product reviews are missing at the top, where trust is built in the first seconds
  • The free gift and packaging add value but don't catch attention
  • Engraving is limited to plain text
Most jewellery research happens online and on mobile. The page has to answer quality, price and trust questions before the buyer scrolls.

Solution

A single, guided page: one clear gallery, reviews and trust badges up front, customisation in four numbered steps, richer engraving and gifting, and a sticky price summary so the final price is never a surprise.

  • Clear lab-grown badge on the gallery
  • Single image with arrow controls and thumbnails
  • Ratings and reviews right after the product title
  • Trust badges for returns, certification, resizing and sourcing
  • Origin and quality grade as separate cards with prices
  • Check marks on every selected option, not colour alone
  • Engraving with symbols and a date, plus premium packaging
  • Sticky price summary and a visible free gift

Process

  1. BriefRead the assignment: audience, objectives and evaluation criteria.
  2. Market researchUS jewellery market size, share by product, material and channel.
  3. User behaviourHow US buyers research, gift and buy, and what worries them.
  4. AuditWalked through the live page on desktop and mobile and noted each gap.
  5. RedesignRebuilt the page section by section and made a working prototype.

Final design

Impact

1 priceOne price, kept current by a sticky summary
4 stepsGemstone, carat, metal and size in a fixed order
3 tiersPackaging from a free box to a keepsake chest

Design assignment. Outcomes not measured.

Learnings

Research on how US buyers shop, mostly on mobile and often for gifts, shaped every decision on the page. Separating origin from grade and adding check marks showed how much clarity comes from small structural fixes rather than new features.

03 · Enterprise operations · 2024

Assam Ferry System: ERP Dashboard

Centralized operations, safety and revenue governance for Inland Water Transport, Assam.

Role
Systems and interface design
Type
Enterprise operations
Year
2024

Interactive case study ↗Working prototype ↗

Overview

Ferries across the Brahmaputra connect Guwahati and North Guwahati. The system is still largely manual, which leads to inefficiency, safety risks and a poor passenger experience.

  • Daily commuters: students, office workers, traders
  • Two-wheelers and light vehicles
  • Tourists and local communities
  • Governance split between public and private operators

Problem

There is no centralized, real-time operational system. The result is overcrowding, delayed decisions, revenue leakage and fragmented safety compliance across terminals.

  • No real-time passenger visibility
  • Overcapacity incidents during peak hours
  • Manual revenue logs, causing mismatch and leakage
  • Safety compliance is reactive, not proactive
  • Information fragmented across terminals
Most operational failures are not due to lack of infrastructure, but lack of centralized visibility.

Solution

A centralized ERP dashboard that lets ferry authorities:

  • Monitor operations in real time
  • Improve safety compliance
  • Digitize ticketing and revenue tracking
  • Make informed decisions with live data

Process

  1. Field researchVisits to Mazgaon, Rajaduar and Madhyamkhanda ghats.
  2. Systems thinkingStakeholder value web and PESTEL analysis.
  3. Analysis80/20 rule, garbage in/garbage out, scaling fallacy, comparison with railways, metro and bus.
  4. DefineTwo personas: a terminal manager and a ferry authority admin.
  5. FlowsAdmin, terminal manager and ticket validation flows.

Final design

Impact

Real timeCapacity, ferry status and safety alerts across terminals
1 systemOperations, ticketing, safety and revenue in one dashboard
Closed loopComplaints tracked from submission to closure

Design outcomes. Not yet measured in deployment.

05 · Mobile · Behaviour design · 2025

Pebble: A habit companion that forgives

Most habit apps reset your streak to zero after one missed day. Pebble lets you repair it, or choose a small punishment, so a bad day doesn't end the habit.

Role
UI/UX design · End to end
Type
Mobile · Behaviour design
Year
2025

Interactive case study ↗Figma prototype ↗

Overview

Developing positive habits is key to growth and well-being, but staying consistent is tough. Having faced this challenge myself, I designed Pebble, a pocket-sized habit companion with a friendly monster guide, to help people stay on track and use their time well.

  • Students: focus, study consistency, exam preparation
  • Young professionals: productivity, career growth, work-life balance
  • Fitness enthusiasts: workouts, active lifestyle, goal tracking
  • Health advocates: routines, mental wellness, sleep and recovery

Problem

People struggle to maintain long-term goals and rely on habit apps for streak-based motivation. When a streak breaks, they feel like failures, and most apps simply send them back to zero.

  • Most users abandon habits after missing 2 to 3 consecutive days
  • 74% say losing streaks feels discouraging; 68% forget to complete habits
  • 57% miss personalised guidance; 45% get too many notifications
  • 80% say personalised reminders would help them stay consistent
  • Existing apps focus on streaks, not recovery
  • Gamification motivates beginners but loses effect over time
Thik karna hoga jo galti hui hai, nahi to saja milegi. Fix the mistake, or take the consequence. That childhood rule became the repair and punishment method.

Solution

Replace the hard reset with a choice. After a missed day, users either repair the streak by building forward, or accept a small punishment that costs double days, and the habit carries on.

  • Onboarding that asks how you react to missing a habit, with a progress bar
  • Choose your streak method: Repair or Punishment
  • Streak cards: green ticks, a red cross that turns green as you repair it
  • Punishment greys out two past days instead of wiping the streak
  • Preset habits to start fast, plus custom habits with goals and reminders
  • Pebble, a monster guide whose pose changes with each habit

Process

  1. Secondary researchCompetitive analysis of Habitica, Fabulous, HabitBull and Streaks, and a market gap map.
  2. Primary researchInterviews with 18 people aged 18 to 35 and a survey of 122 respondents.
  3. DefineEmpathy map, two personas, affinity mapping and the Hook Model.
  4. User flowFrom onboarding and login to daily check-in, skip, progress and insights.
  5. Low fidelityPaper sketches and grey wireframes for onboarding, habits, repair and punishment.
  6. TestUsability tests with two users, then fixed spacing, readability and navigation.

Final design

Impact

122Survey respondents, plus 18 interviews
0 resetsA missed day is repaired or punished, never wiped
2 methodsRepair or Punishment, chosen at onboarding

Tested with 2 users. Not yet measured in production.

Learnings

The emotion after a missed day mattered more than any feature. Designing for recovery, and letting people pick how strict to be with themselves, made the app feel like a companion rather than a scoreboard. Testing showed small things still matter: readable text, spacing and clear icons.

06 · E-commerce · CRO · 2026

Yourbasil: A product page that fills the cart

70% of Basil customers buy only one product per order. I redesigned the product detail page so the reasons to add a second and third item appear before the customer decides, not after.

Role
UX design task
Type
E-commerce · CRO
Year
2026

Interactive case study ↗Live site ↗

Overview

Basil sells lunch boxes, water bottles, food jars, school bags, plates, pickle and dip containers and gift products for kids. The brief: 70% of customers on yourbasil.com purchase only one product per order, which directly impacts Average Order Value. Using the product detail page as the canvas, design at least one section that upsells or cross-sells, and explain why this section, why this placement and which behaviour or CRO principle it is built on.

  • Parents buying for school-age kids, often for siblings
  • Buying moments: school opening, new school year, festivals, birthdays and gifting
  • Most bought together: a lunch box and a water bottle
  • Canvas: the Bento Ace product page, Spider-Man drop

Problem

The current page sells one product well: price, vibe picker, a personalisation toggle and Add to cart. But nothing on the page pushes a second item, and the offers that would reward it sit in the wrong place.

  • Buy 2 get 5% and Buy 3 get 10% are only revealed in the cart, after one item is added
  • The quantity decision comes before the offers, so they never shape it
  • The set price and savings exist, but in the side cart, when the customer has mentally checked out
  • Product features repeat the badges at the top and push related products far down
  • Explore more needs too much scrolling to reach
  • Complete the Kit cards are small and cut off at the edge
The discount logic lives in the cart, not the product page. Customers only discover it after they've added one item. Too late.

Solution

Bring the offer logic onto the product page and place every cross-sell where buying momentum is highest: right around Add to cart.

  • How many boxes: three quantity cards with the extra discount, per-box price and savings, with Best value on 3
  • Mix vibes for siblings: set a quantity for each design
  • Personalise for ₹149 per box, with an instant name preview on the lid
  • Complete the Kit below Add to cart: a matching bottle and backpack, each counting toward the offer
  • A progress bar and live order summary showing the next discount tier
  • Frequently bought together: a set of 3 with a set price, a free sticker sheet and one Add all 3 to cart button

Process

  1. Understand AOVAOV is total revenue divided by orders. Higher-value products in each order means a higher AOV.
  2. Product rangeLunch boxes, bottles, food jars, bags, plates, dips and gifts: what can pair with what.
  3. Upsell and cross-sellLunch box plus lunch box is an upsell. Lunch box plus water bottle is a cross-sell.
  4. Customer behaviourWhat parents buy together and when: school opening, festivals, birthdays and gifting.
  5. CRO principlesClear product information, a strong CTA, reviews, upsell, cross-sell, easy checkout and trust signals.
  6. Audit and redesignAnnotated the live page, then rebuilt the buy box and added Frequently bought together.

Final design

Impact

On the PDP5% and 10% offers shown before the quantity decision, not in the cart
3 leversQuantity cards, Complete the Kit and Frequently bought together
1 tapAdd a full set of 3 to cart

Design task. Principles used: default bias, anchoring and loss aversion. Outcomes not measured.

Learnings

Placement mattered more than the component. The same offers already existed in the cart; moving them to the moment of decision, and letting every item count toward them, is what turns a one-item visit into a kit.
